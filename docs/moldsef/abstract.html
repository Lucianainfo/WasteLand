WasteLand: does a price that falls toward closing time rescue more surplus food and more revenue? A pre-registered simulation benchmark and venue cost model

Luciana Posturusu (Grade 12) · Supervisor: Azamat Jayush · Software Development / Food waste

1. Aim and hypotheses

About 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022, and 19% of the food available to consumers ends up in the bin [1]. WasteLand lists a venue's unsold food in the last three hours before closing and lowers its price as closing approaches: the discount rises from 30% to 70% along p1.3 (p is the share of the window elapsed), plus up to 5 points when much of the batch is still unsold. The exponent 1.3 and the boost were design choices, not results. We tested whether this rule beats the two simple rules a venue would otherwise use. The hypotheses, decision rule and demand scenarios were written on 2026-11-12, before the simulator was run (repository file research/PREREGISTRATION.md, SHA-256 c209c8e4051d… recorded in the results). The targets were proposed by the supervisor and not changed afterwards. H1a/H1b: compared with a fixed 50% discount (A) or a linear 30%→70% markdown (B), the rule (C) raises the share of portions sold by at least 25%. H2a/H2b: it raises revenue recovered per evening by at least 18%. A hypothesis is supported only if the estimate reaches the target and the 95% interval excludes zero; if the interval is above zero but the estimate is below the target, it is partially supported.

2. Methodology

Simulator. One trial is one venue-evening with 2–8 unsold portions of a catalogue item. Interested customers arrive as a Poisson process (D = 0.75, 1.5, 3 per portion over the 180-minute window) at random minutes; each is willing to pay a share w of the full price, w ~ Normal(m, 0.20) truncated to [0.05, 1] with m = 0.35, 0.45, 0.55, and buys one portion if the displayed price is at or below that. A share of 30% are patient: they keep checking every minute and buy once the price has fallen enough, so a falling price is not rewarded by assuming nobody notices it [4]. The price is recomputed from the current stock before every decision, using the deployed functions discountFor and discountedPrice imported from the server code. Design. Nine demand scenarios (D × m), 2,000 trials each, 18,000 in total (the requirement is N ≥ 100); every rule sells to the same customers (paired design). Rule A2, a fixed 67% discount (one third of the value, the typical surprise-bag price [2]), was added for context and is not part of any hypothesis. Statistics. Pooled relative improvement with a paired 2,000-resample bootstrap [5]; seed wl|v1, used once (debugging used another seed). Limits. There is no real sales data: the arrival rate, willingness to pay and patience are assumptions, bracketed by the grid and six one-at-a-time variations, not measured.

All four hypotheses are partially supported. Against the fixed 50% discount the rule rescues 19.0% more portions (95% interval 18.4% to 19.7%, target 25%) and recovers 11.1% more revenue (10.6% to 11.6%, target 18%), in 9 of 9 scenarios. Against the linear markdown the difference is real but tiny: +0.6% portions (0.5% to 0.8%) and +0.4% revenue (0.2% to 0.5%), better in only 5 and 6 of 9 scenarios, because the two share their endpoints. Why 1.3? Sweeping the exponent from 0.5 to 3.0 shows a plateau, not a sharp optimum: revenue peaks at γ = 1.3, but every exponent from 1.0 to 1.6 is within 0.1% of it, and 1.3 is not statistically separable from 1.0 or 1.6. The ablation shows which ingredient works: the curve alone (γ = 1.3, no boost) is worse than the straight line (−3.1% portions, −0.3% revenue); the boost for unsold stock adds the rescued portions (+3.7% on the straight line). Against the fixed 67% discount the rule rescues 13.3% fewer portions but recovers 23.2% more revenue, and customers keep 50% less surplus: last-call pricing maximises money, a deep discount maximises food rescued. Sensitivity: the advantage over the fixed 50% discount depends on customers noticing the falling price. If nobody waits, it rescues +2.0% portions but earns −5.2% revenue, less than the fixed discount; if 60% wait, +31.1% and +24.0%. Medium-demand scenarios rescue 57%–82% of portions (the demo network assumes 65%).

4. Economic and social applicability

Venue ROI. Reference bakery: 15 unsold items a day at 60 MDL (27,000 MDL of retail value a month), €1 = 20.1 MDL (BNM, early November 2026 [6]), 30 days a month. The food is already baked, so recovered revenue is pure gain. Thrown away it brings 0. With the benchmark's yields the rule recovers 9,012 MDL a month (€448), 108,148 MDL (€5,380) a year, 33% of retail value, and rescues 10.7 items a day (160 kg a month); the fixed 50% discount recovers 8,136 MDL a month and the linear markdown 8,969 MDL. A surprise-bag app selling at 67% off keeps 5,499 MDL a month after a 25% commission and 5,133 MDL after 30%; a commission above about 19% would make an uncharged fixed 67% discount worth more than WasteLand. The 25–30% rates come from the supervisor and could not be confirmed on Too Good To Go's own pages [2]. If binning costs €0.10 per kg (a placeholder, not a source), a further €16 a month is saved. Cost of running WasteLand. The prototype costs 0 on Vercel's Hobby plan and Upstash's free tier, but Hobby is limited to personal, non-commercial use, so a pilot that charges venues needs Vercel Pro (USD 20 a month excluding VAT) [7]. Counting the Redis commands in the code, the globe endpoint alone spends 56 commands per 60-second cache refresh, so steady traffic passes Upstash's 500,000 free commands a month: 342,500 commands (€18.52 a month) for 5,000 visits at steady evening traffic [8]. Social value: 2.82 kg of food per venue-evening rescued instead of binned. Unverified: commission rates, the disposal placeholder and all demand parameters.

5. Conclusions

In this simulation, last-call pricing rescues about a fifth more portions and about a tenth more revenue than a fixed 50% discount, short of the pre-registered 25% and 18% targets; it is practically indistinguishable from a plain linear markdown, and 1.3 is one point on a flat plateau rather than a proven optimum. The unsold-stock boost, not the curve, is what adds rescued portions; a deep fixed discount rescues more food but returns less money. The result holds only if some customers notice and wait for the price to fall. The missing experiment is a pilot with one real bakery: measure arrivals and willingness to pay, then re-run this benchmark with those numbers as an amendment.
